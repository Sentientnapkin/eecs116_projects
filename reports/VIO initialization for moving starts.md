# Anchor velocity and tilt in the first 50 ms

For a moving start, the best initialization for a small MSCKF is to treat frame 0 as a filter state with honest, wide uncertainty rather than wait for a batch initializer. Inject a calibrated learned velocity measurement at frame 1 instead of frame 5, solve gravity and initial velocity jointly from that velocity and integrated IMU instead of reading tilt off the accelerometer, and protect the first second against the three ways early filters lock in bad estimates: overconfident covariance, FEJ linearization points frozen at a bad first estimate, and chi-square gates that reject the measurements that would correct the state. Classical visual-inertial initializers need 1.25–2.5 s of data and mostly assume known biases. The fastest published closed form uses a 0.48 s window at about 1 ms. Learned priors used only during startup have cut downstream position error by 84% on EuRoC. Our filter already has the most valuable ingredient, a learned two-frame velocity CNN, but it applies it too late (frame 5) and pairs it with a zero-mean velocity prior that pulls early speed, and so scale, toward zero. The highest-return changes are all cheap: moving the first CNN call earlier, recalibrating its covariance, a few-unknown least-squares fit for gravity and initial velocity, and startup-only gating and FEJ hygiene. Expensive options such as filter banks, full MLE and learned depth either add one-off compute spikes that the log-scale per-frame metric punishes, or duplicate what the CNN already provides.

## Batch initializers need 0.5–2.5 s and an SfM step we lack

Every visual-inertial initializer in the literature solves the same observability problem. Initial velocity, gravity direction and metric scale become observable only once the window contains enough acceleration and parallax. They differ mainly in what they assume and what they cost. The Martinelli family of closed forms solves velocity, gravity and feature depths in one linear system, with no structure-from-motion, but assumes the biases are known. Kaiser et al. showed that unmodeled gyro bias "significantly affects" accuracy, and fixing it makes the problem iterative ([UZH Merlin](https://www.merlin.uzh.ch/publication/show/13323)). Campos et al. measured about **60 ms** for the Martinelli–Kaiser solve, and their own earlier joint method worked at only **about 20% of trajectory points** ([Campos et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.05766)). In a later evaluation of that earlier work, the closed form gave **32.9–156.7% scale error** on EuRoC V1 before bundle adjustment ([arXiv 1908.10653](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10653)).

The optimization-based pipelines are more accurate, but they are built around keyframe SLAM. VINS-Mono fills a 10-keyframe window and requires more than 20 correspondences and about 30 px of parallax at a 460 px virtual focal length before its SfM runs. It solves gyro bias first and then velocity, gravity and scale linearly, and ignores accelerometer bias ([VINS-Mono](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.03852); [estimator.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HKUST-Aerial-Robotics/VINS-Mono/master/vins_estimator/src/estimator.cpp)). A re-benchmark over 2,248 EuRoC trials puts it at **0.11 m/s velocity error and 1.47° gravity error for 30.94 ms**, almost all of it SfM ([Cerezo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18910v2)). In Campos et al.'s test, though, it had **22% mean scale error**, with many solutions near zero scale ([Campos et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.05766)). ORB-SLAM3's inertial-only MAP step is cheap at about 5 ms per scale seed, but it sits on 2.16 s of monocular SLAM. It reaches **5.3% scale error at 2.16 s and 9.1% at 1.25 s**, and a total of **about 160 ms** once VI-BA is included ([Campos et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.05766)).

Two recent lines matter more for an embedded MSCKF. Rotation–translation decoupling estimates gyro bias from rotations alone, then velocity and gravity linearly without 3-D points. DRT-VIO runs in **5.18 ms over a 2.5 s window** ([He et al. CVPR 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/html/He_A_Rotation-Translation-Decoupled_Solution_for_Robust_and_Efficient_Visual-Inertial_Initialization_CVPR_2023_paper.html); [Cerezo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18910v2)). Cerezo, Lee and Civera's 2025 closed form estimates every state, including both biases. It uses an adaptive window of **0.48 ± 0.27 s at 0.96 ms total**, with 0.09 m/s velocity and 0.90° gravity error, and its gyro-bias step takes 19–94 µs ([arXiv 2511.18910](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18910v2)). It assumes small rotation and constant angular velocity over the window, which a rotating drone can violate. OpenVINS's dynamic initializer is the closest existing fit to our architecture. It runs a Dong-Si–style linear solve for features, v0 and gravity under the constraint |g| = 9.81, followed by a Ceres MLE capped at 0.05 s. With the shipped EuRoC config it needs a 2 s window, 6 poses and at least 10° of rotation, and it is disabled by default ([OpenVINS config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rpng/open_vins/master/config/euroc_mav/estimator_config.yaml); [DynamicInitializer.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rpng/open_vins/master/ov_init/src/dynamic/DynamicInitializer.cpp)).

| Method | Window | Reported cost | Biases | Fit for our MSCKF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VINS-Mono alignment | ~1.3–2 s, needs 30 px parallax | 30.9 ms (SfM-dominated) | b_g only | Poor: needs SfM |
| ORB-SLAM3 inertial-only + VI-BA | 2.16 s | ~160 ms | both, b_a prior | Poor: needs SLAM map |
| OpenVINS dynamic (linear + MLE) | 2 s, ≥10° rotation | ≤50 ms MLE | fixed in linear stage | Linear stage only |

| Learned-depth init (Merrill 2023) | 0.3–0.5 s | ~0.2 s CPU depth net | via MLE | Too costly per call |

Two conclusions follow for a mid-flight start. First, **non-zero initial velocity is never the obstacle**: v0 is an explicit unknown in every one of these linear systems. What breaks them is near-constant velocity with little parallax. Campos and Zuñiga-Noël reject windows whose mean acceleration is below 0.5% of g ([Zuñiga-Noël 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03389)). Second, **accelerometer bias is effectively unobservable in short windows**, with errors at or above 100% even with ground-truth poses ([Zuñiga-Noël 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03389)), so the practical choice is to fix b_a at zero with a prior. Direction of travel matters only through parallax. Sideways flight gives the most parallax; forward or backward flight along the optical axis gives the least near the focus of expansion, so a drone moving backwards starves parallax-gated methods longest. Our velocity CNN does not depend on that gate, which makes it the better primary startup signal.

## Filter-side startup hygiene matters more than the initializer

The reason a few bad starts dominate our mean is that an EKF that starts wrong usually stays wrong. Four mechanisms account for most of this, and each has a cheap remedy.

**Covariance has to be honest in both directions.** If P is too small, the filter down-weights or gates out correct measurements and stays inconsistent. If it is too large, the first updates make large jumps computed from poorly linearized Jacobians. OpenVINS recovers a covariance from its MLE and then inflates it: ×10 on orientation and gyro bias and ×100 on velocity and accelerometer bias. Its maintainers confirm that short-window covariances come out overconfident ([OpenVINS config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rpng/open_vins/master/config/euroc_mav/estimator_config.yaml); [issue #433](https://www.github.com/rpng/open_vins/issues/433)). Flight stacks behave the same way. PX4 gates tilt initialization on accelerometer norm within 0.8–1.2 g and gyro rate below 15°/s, then assumes the vehicle is static ([PX4 ekf.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PX4/PX4-Autopilot/main/src/modules/ekf2/EKF/ekf.cpp)). ArduPilot takes tilt from a single accelerometer sample with variance (0.1 rad)², starts with both bias states inhibited, and leaves horizontal accelerometer bias unlearned until the vehicle accelerates horizontally ([ArduPilot EKF3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArduPilot/ardupilot/master/libraries/AP_NavEKF3/AP_NavEKF3_core.cpp)). The arithmetic shows why tilt is the critical state for us. Reading gravity from the accelerometer while the vehicle accelerates horizontally at 1 m/s² gives about 5.8° of tilt error. That error leaks g·sin(5.8°) ≈ 1 m/s² of spurious horizontal acceleration, about 0.5 m of position error after one second and 2 m after two. That is the size of our worst episodes. Even 1° of tilt error, about 0.17 m/s², is 0.34 m after 2 s.

**FEJ freezes bad first estimates.** Standard EKF-VINS gains spurious yaw information from Jacobians evaluated at changing estimates. FEJ and observability-constrained Jacobians fix this, and MSCKF 2.0 with FEJ beat a sliding-window iterative smoother of comparable cost on accuracy and consistency ([Li & Mourikis IJRR 2013](https://intra.ece.ucr.edu/~mourikis/papers/Li2013IJRR.pdf); [Hesch et al. TRO 2014](https://fling.seas.upenn.edu/~afosr/wiki/uploads/Chaserepository/Repository/UMN-ST-TRO-VINS-Consistency.pdf)). The catch is in the name. FEJ linearizes at the first estimate, and FEJ2 was written because FEJ degrades when that first estimate is poor ([FEJ2, ICRA 2022](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/9811831)). In our MSCKF, every clone created before velocity and tilt converge carries a bad linearization point for as long as it stays in the window. The fix costs essentially nothing: after the first large correction (the first CNN velocity update, or the gravity/velocity solve below), reset the FEJ values of all existing clones to their current estimates. Alternatively, run with standard Jacobians for the first N frames and switch on FEJ afterwards. Invariant-EKF error states avoid the problem structurally, since their linearized error dynamics do not depend on the estimate ([Barrau & Bonnabel 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.1465)), and RI-MSCKF clearly beat MSCKF on consistency in Monte-Carlo tests ([arXiv 1702.07920](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.07920)). Neither paper reports startup-specific ATE, though, and an invariant formulation is a refactor rather than a tweak.

**Iteration helps exactly where the prior is worst.** An iterated EKF update is Gauss–Newton on the single-update MAP cost ([Bell & Cathey 1993](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/220763)). ROVIO and FAST-LIO use it routinely to absorb linearization error ([Bloesch et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0278364917728574); [FAST-LIO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.08196.pdf)). Iterating a full MSCKF update multiplies its cost. Iterating our CNN velocity update does not, because it has only three measurement rows. Two or three iterations of that update during the first second relinearize the body-to-world rotation that maps CNN velocity into the state, at negligible cost. Iterating feature updates should be limited to frames where the normalized innovation is large. Neither ROVIO, FAST-LIO nor any MSCKF paper we found reports numeric iterated-versus-single-pass ablations for startup.

**Gates lock the filter out.** A χ² gate built on S = HPHᵀ + R is too loose when P is inflated and too tight when P is overconfident. Right after an overconfident hand-off it rejects the measurements that would repair the state ([issue #433](https://www.github.com/rpng/open_vins/issues/433)). At startup, reject outliers with state-independent geometry first: gyro-compensated two-point RANSAC and forward-backward track checks. Then apply the χ² gate with a 3–5× multiplier that decays as trace(P) shrinks. A per-frame rejection rate above roughly 30–50% should be read as filter inconsistency (inflate P), not as outliers. None of the codebases surveyed publishes a "relaxed gate for the first N frames" schedule, so this rule is our inference and should be tuned locally.

The remaining classical tools rank lower for us. Partial updates, which apply only a fraction of the correction to weakly observable states such as biases, limit early linearization damage in Jackson et al.'s VI-EKF ([Jackson et al. ICRA 2019](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8794164/metrics)). Combined with ArduPilot-style bias freezing, they are a free default for the first 1–2 s. The Schmidt-KF ([Geneva et al. ICRA 2019](https://pgeneva.com/downloads/papers/Geneva2019ICRA.pdf)) solves a map-size problem we do not have. Undelayed inverse-depth features ([Civera et al. TRO 2008](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/4637878)) would deliver visual information from the second observation instead of after an MSCKF track completes, which at 20 Hz with 5–11-frame tracks means 0.25–0.55 s of no visual updates. That gap is real, but each SLAM feature costs state dimension, and the CNN already fills the gap more cheaply. Filter banks over initial velocity, with K full hypotheses weighted by innovation likelihood, have no published VIO results we could find. They multiply per-frame cost by K, which is the wrong trade under a log-scale compute score when one calibrated CNN measurement resolves the same ambiguity. Fixed-lag smoothing over the first second, or re-propagating the window once velocity and bias are known, is the filter analogue of the OpenVINS hand-off. It produces a one-off spike unless it is amortized over several frames.

## Quadrotor drag gives free velocity, if the simulator models it

On a real quadrotor, thrust acts along body z, so the horizontal specific force is dominated by rotor drag, roughly proportional to −v_body. A linear drag model therefore makes horizontal body velocity observable from the IMU alone, before any visual track matures ([Leishman et al. 2014](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6712082)). Abeywardena et al. fused this model with epipolar constraints and obtained drift-free velocity and attitude despite biases, on a 50 g computer ([arXiv 1607.01486](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.01486)). Jackson et al. found that a linear drag term improved VI-EKF accuracy ([Jackson et al. 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10135575)). As a pseudo-measurement it is a 2-row update and costs almost nothing, and because v_body = Rᵀv_world it also constrains tilt. There are three caveats. It measures airspeed, not ground speed. Aggressive flight adds unmodeled terms; HDVIO learns a hybrid model because pure drag models fail in fast flight ([arXiv 2306.11429](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.11429v2)). Most importantly for us, **if our simulated flights do not generate rotor drag in the accelerometer, this measurement carries no information and will bias the state.** Check the simulator's IMU model with a one-line regression of body-xy specific force against ground-truth body velocity before implementing anything. If the slope is near zero, drop the idea.

## The velocity CNN is our best initializer if its uncertainty is honest

The strongest published evidence for startup-only learned priors comes from depth. Zhou et al. added learned monocular depth to a 0.4 s initialization and **cut downstream 10 s position RMSE on EuRoC from 2.24 m to 0.35 m (−84%)** and gravity error by 47%. Successful initializations rose from 1,078 to 1,547 of 1,680 attempts ([Zhou et al. ECCV 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.09171)). Merrill et al. used MiDaS-small to reduce 0.5 s-window scale error from 57.6% to 5.8% and initialized from 0.3 s on TUM-VI ([Merrill et al. RSS 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10479088)). The depth networks themselves are expensive, 71.6 ms on a phone CPU ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.09171)), so for us a learned depth prior would be a large one-off spike. Our CNN plays the same role more directly: it measures velocity, and therefore scale, from two frames. We found no paper that uses a learned velocity network specifically for VIO initialization, so the comparison is by analogy.

How the learned output is fused decides whether it helps. TLIO's lesson is that the regressed covariance, more than per-sample accuracy, made its EKF beat velocity integration (**−33% position drift** versus RoNIN-style concatenation). Fixed-covariance variants underperformed even when tuned ([TLIO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01867)). TLIO's recipe transfers directly. Train with MSE, then switch to Gaussian NLL, because NLL from scratch did not converge. Perturb inputs with filter-realistic bias and gravity errors, because inputs are "potentially inaccurate [...] especially at the initialization stage". Validate with 3σ coverage (over 99%) and χ² exceedance (0.30% beyond the 99% point). Gate in the filter at χ²₃ = 11.345 ([TLIO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01867)). DIDO still rescales NLL covariances in the filter because linearization distorts them ([DIDO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.03149)). That supports one global inflation factor tuned on held-out flights, plus a second factor for the first update, when tilt is least certain. For drones specifically, AirIO's body-frame velocity with an attitude encoding generalized to unseen Blackbird trajectories at **1.31 m ATE, versus 9.0 m for the world-frame IMO**. Body-frame representation mattered more as models shrank: its 44k-parameter variant lost 44% accuracy in the body frame against 82% in the global frame ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659v2)). Our 1.8 M-MAC CNN is in this small-model regime.

Two fusion errors are specific to our setup. First, the zero-mean isotropic velocity prior is the wrong shape for a moving start. A drone in cruise is almost never at zero velocity, so a zero-mean Gaussian pulls every early estimate toward zero speed. That shrinks scale until the first CNN update arrives at frame 5. An isotropic prior is correct about direction, since the drone can be flying backward or sideways, but its variance should reflect the training-set speed distribution, and its job should end at frame 1. Second, consecutive CNN outputs computed from overlapping frames have correlated errors. TLIO found that overlapping windows did not lower ATE for exactly this reason ([TLIO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01867)). Several early calls therefore need inflated covariance or wide spacing, and the CNN output should be used either as a repeated measurement or as an initial prior, never both. The evidence also favors using the CNN heavily at startup and less once vision is well-conditioned. IMO-style learned odometry lost to OpenVINS on easy Blackbird tracks (0.22 m vs 0.12 m on Winter) while winning where vision failed ([IMO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.15287v2)). No paper directly compares startup-only use with continuous use in one filter.

## Recipes ranked by gain per unit of compute

The first two seconds should run as a fixed schedule. **At frame 0**, set tilt from the accelerometer averaged over the IMU samples available, with σ ≈ 0.1 rad (the ArduPilot and PX4 figure), not tighter. Fix yaw and position as the gauge. Set biases to zero and freeze them. Give velocity a broad prior matched to the speed distribution. **At frame 1 (50 ms)**, run the CNN on frames 0–1 and apply its velocity with the calibrated NLL covariance times a startup inflation factor, using 2–3 iterated updates. Then reset clone FEJ points. **From frame 2 to about frame 10**, let MSCKF line and point updates begin as tracks mature, with state-independent outlier rejection and a 3–5× χ² multiplier that decays with trace(P). Bias states stay frozen or receive partial updates. **At frames 5 and 10**, the second and third CNN velocities plus gyro-integrated, rotated accelerometer increments give a 4–7-unknown least-squares fit for gravity and v0. This is the aerospace optimization-based-alignment idea ([Wu & Pan 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1550)) with learned velocity in place of GPS. Its job is to correct tilt and catch a bad first CNN output, and it costs microseconds. **Over 1–2 s**, release the gyro bias first, then accelerometer bias only after horizontal excitation. Return the gate multiplier and the CNN inflation to steady-state values. If the episode has rotated more than about 10° and the CNN and gravity fits disagree, a Cerezo-style closed-form solve (~1 ms, spread over frames) acts as an independent check.

Gains in the table are qualitative expectations. None of the sources reports ATE over the first 0.5–3 s for these techniques, so each must be measured locally. Costs are relative to our per-frame budget.

To measure any of this, copy the Campos protocol: launch an initialization every 0.5 s along each training flight, and report success rate, velocity and tilt error at 1 s and 2 s, and ATE at fixed horizons ([Campos et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.05766)). This turns our few bad-start episodes into thousands of moving-start trials and shows directly which of ranks 1–8 moves the tail.

## Conclusion

The literature on initialization is mostly about getting scale out of geometry when nothing else provides it, and it pays for that with 0.5–2.5 s of waiting and an SfM or batch solve. A filter with a calibrated learned velocity measurement has already paid that cost at training time. For us the startup problem is therefore less about choosing an initializer and more about not wasting or corrupting the information the CNN provides. Waiting four frames, holding a zero-velocity prior, taking tilt from an accelerating accelerometer, freezing FEJ at pre-correction estimates, and gating against an overconfident P are each small decisions. Together they are a plausible explanation for why a few starts end up meters off while the Sim(3)-aligned error stays near 0.29 m.

The open risks are empirical. We found no published numbers for any of these techniques measured over the first seconds, no study of a learned velocity network used as an initializer, and no confirmation that our simulator produces the rotor drag that would make the cheapest physical prior useful. The CNN's calibration, measured as χ² exceedance on held-out mid-flight starts, is the single number most worth computing first. If it shows the CNN is overconfident in exactly the bad-start episodes, recalibrating its covariance will matter more than anything on the list that changes the filter.
